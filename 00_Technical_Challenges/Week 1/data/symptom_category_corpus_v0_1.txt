PEDIATRIC CONCERN CATEGORIES: DESCRIPTIONS BY AGE (CORPUS v0.1, DRAFT)
Purpose: map a caregiver's description of a child to concern categories, with age-specific information. This is synthetic-data research material. It is not clinically reviewed and not for diagnosis.
Everything below is paraphrased from public health, clinical and education sources. Source lists are kept in a separate provenance file, not in this document.
Structure: 15 categories. Each has an overview section, then four age bands: under 4 years, 4 to 5 years, 6 to 11 years, 12 to 17 years. Every section starts with a header line naming its category and section or age band.
Where the sources say little for an age band, the section says so plainly instead of guessing.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 1: ATTENTION AND EXECUTIVE FUNCTION (attention_executive)
##########################################################################

Category: attention_executive | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: difficulty paying attention, staying on task, controlling impulses, sitting still, and the planning, organizing, remembering and self-monitoring skills known as executive function. Executive function is described as the set of mental skills for holding information in mind, focusing attention, filtering distractions, switching between tasks, setting goals, planning, checking progress, adjusting plans and managing frustration. Three parts are named: working memory (holding information in mind and using it), inhibitory control (resisting impulses, distractions and habits, and pausing before acting), and cognitive flexibility (adjusting to changing demands or perspectives). These skills are not present at birth. Children are born with the potential to develop them, and they build slowly from infancy into early adulthood.
How caregivers often describe it: "he can't sit still", "she never listens", "he loses everything", "she can't finish her homework", "he daydreams all day", "he blurts out answers", "he is always on the go", "she can't wait her turn", "he forgets his chores and assignments", "he starts things and never finishes them", "she is so disorganized", "he is careless with his work".
Three patterns are described for ADHD. Mostly inattentive: trouble organizing or finishing tasks, missing details, following instructions or conversations, being easily distracted, forgetting daily routines. Mostly hyperactive and impulsive: fidgeting, talking a lot, trouble sitting through meals or homework, interrupting, grabbing things, speaking at the wrong time, trouble waiting a turn, more accidents and injuries. Combined: both. The pattern can change over time as symptoms change.
Normal versus concerning: it is normal for children to have trouble focusing and behaving at times. With ADHD the problems continue, can be severe, and cause difficulty at school, at home or with friends. Clinicians look for symptoms before age 12, in two or more settings, lasting at least six months, and getting in the way of social, school or work functioning. These counts are for clinicians and are not a checklist to put to caregivers.
Often overlaps with or is confused with: sleep disorders, anxiety, depression, certain learning disabilities, stress and physical conditions. ADHD also often occurs alongside anxiety, depression, conduct problems, oppositional behavior, learning and language disorders, autism, tics and sleep problems. A medical exam with hearing and vision checks is part of the usual process because other problems can look like ADHD. There is no single test. Diagnosis is made by a primary care provider or mental health professional using information from caregivers, teachers and other adults about behavior in different settings, plus rating scales. Teacher input is described as essential at all ages.

Category: attention_executive | Age band: under 4 years
The pediatric guideline for ADHD covers evaluation from age 4. It says there is not enough evidence to diagnose or treat ADHD in children under 4, and that for a child under 4 with substantial impairment from ADHD-like behavior, referral for parent training in behavior management can be considered. For children under 3, a local early intervention program can provide a free or low-cost evaluation without a doctor's referral.
Expected development: from 6 to 18 months infants are building basic attention, working memory and self-control, and responsive interaction with adults is the foundation. From 18 to 36 months, fast language growth helps children name their thoughts and actions and follow more complex rules, and toddlers are developing the ability to keep attention on a goal, hold back unhelpful actions and try a new strategy when the first one fails.
Smaller children with a hyperactive-impulsive pattern may run, jump or climb constantly. At 3 years, a typical milestone is calming down within about 10 minutes after a parent leaves, such as at child care drop-off.
Useful to find out: how much the behavior gets in the way of daily life, whether it happens with other caregivers and in child care, whether hearing and vision have been checked, how the child sleeps, and whether early intervention has been contacted.

Category: attention_executive | Age band: 4 to 5 years
An ADHD evaluation can start from age 4 when a child has behavior or learning problems together with inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity. For preschool-aged children the evaluation uses a caregiver interview, direct observation of the child, and rating scales from caregivers and teachers. Preschoolers who are not in child care or preschool may show symptoms that are hard to see across settings, and early intervention programs or parent-training groups can offer observations from other settings. Norms for ADHD rating scales based on current criteria exist from age 5.
Expected development from 3 to 5 years: self-regulation grows quickly. Children learn to plan before acting, coordinate with peers and begin to regulate each other's behavior. Working memory, flexibility and planning develop through role play and storytelling. A typical milestone at 5 years is paying attention for 5 to 10 minutes during an activity (screen time does not count). At 5 years typical children also follow rules and take turns in games with other children.
In children generally, hyperactivity and impulsivity are the most common ADHD symptoms. Smaller children with this pattern may run, jump or climb constantly.
Useful to find out: where the behavior shows up (home, preschool, with peers), whether another adult who sees the child regularly has noticed the same things, how long the child can stay with a favorite activity, and whether concerns about language, learning or other development sit alongside.

Category: attention_executive | Age band: 6 to 11 years
As academic and social demands grow, inattention often becomes more prominent and can interfere with schoolwork and peer relationships, while hyperactivity and impulsivity are the most common symptoms in children overall. Symptoms can increase when the demands of daily life increase. Sitting through meals and doing homework are named as common trouble spots.
Listed inattention behaviors include careless mistakes, trouble concentrating in conversations, tests and reading, seeming not to listen, trouble following instructions or finishing tasks, trouble organizing and managing time, avoiding tasks that need sustained mental effort such as homework, frequently losing things, being easily distracted and forgetting daily obligations such as chores or assignments. Listed hyperactive and impulsive behaviors include fidgeting or squirming, leaving a seat when expected to stay, running or climbing at the wrong times, seeming constantly on the go, being unable to play quietly, talking excessively, blurting out answers, struggling to wait a turn and interrupting.
Expected development: from 5 to 7 years children enjoy rule-based games and can apply rules with less adult help, and games and puzzles exercise attention, working memory and flexible thinking. From 7 to 12 years working memory, flexible thinking and planning become more complex, and selective attention and self-monitoring matter more in sports, music and dance.
The guideline notes that differences between classrooms and teachers can give useful information, and school supports such as an IEP or 504 plan can be part of care. Symptoms must have begun before age 12.
Useful to find out: what is seen at home (homework, meals, routines) versus what teachers report, whether concerns differ between classes or teachers, which behaviors are present, when it began, whether it got worse as school demands grew, and how peer relationships and schoolwork are affected.

Category: attention_executive | Age band: 12 to 17 years
In teens, hyperactivity is usually less pronounced and may show as restlessness or fidgeting, while inattention and impulsivity typically continue and can cause academic, organizational or relationship problems. Teens are more likely to take risks, including substance use. Hyperactivity tends to ease as a child becomes a teen, while inattention, disorganization and poor impulse control often continue.
Expected development: executive function is not yet adult level, but demands are high. Teens are expected to set and pursue meaningful goals, monitor their own behavior, reflect on setbacks, resist distractions, and organize their work largely on their own, including breaking projects into parts and tracking deadlines.
For evaluation, reports should come from at least two teachers or other sources such as coaches, counselors or activity leaders, and gathering teacher input is hard when a teen has many teachers. Teens' own reports often differ from other observers' because teens tend to minimize problems, and the teen's agreement to the evaluation is essential. A teen not previously diagnosed must show ADHD behavior from before age 12, so childhood history and old records matter. At minimum the guideline asks for screening for substance use, anxiety, depression and learning disabilities, and asks clinicians to consider marijuana effects, trauma and toxic stress as things that can look like ADHD or occur with it.
Useful to find out: what the caregiver sees and what the teen says, where they differ; which adults outside the family see the teen; whether attention or activity concerns were present before age 12 and what earlier teachers said; organization, deadlines and schoolwork; mood, worry, sleep, substance use and major stress.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 2: LEARNING AND ACADEMIC SKILLS (learning_academic)
##########################################################################

Category: learning_academic | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: difficulty learning to read, spell, write or do math at the level expected for age. Learning disabilities are described as differences in the brain that affect how well a person reads, writes, speaks, does math and handles similar tasks. Types named: dyslexia (trouble reading words accurately and fluently, spelling, understanding sentences and recognizing familiar words), dysgraphia (trouble with handwriting, forming letters, writing within a space, and writing down thoughts) and dyscalculia (trouble understanding number concepts and doing arithmetic, measuring and estimating time). Dyslexia is described as language-based, affecting reading, spelling, speaking and writing, and it has nothing to do with intelligence.
How caregivers often describe it: "she's smart but can't read", "he reads slowly and guesses at words", "she can't remember her letters and sounds", "his spelling is all over the place", "he reverses letters", "his handwriting is unreadable and he hates writing", "she can't get her ideas onto paper", "he still counts on his fingers", "he can't memorize math facts", "she can't tell time on a clock", "he avoids reading and homework", "the teacher says he's behind".
Often overlaps with or is confused with: attention problems (children often have both), language disorders, slow processing speed, motor coordination difficulties (a slow messy handwriting pattern is linked to developmental coordination disorder and dysgraphia), visual-spatial difficulties (which affect math layout, copying and reading comprehension that depends on pictures), anxiety, and hearing or vision problems. General signs listed by NIH include problems with reading, writing or math, poor memory and problems paying attention.

Category: learning_academic | Age band: under 4 years
The sources describe formal learning signs mostly from preschool age. For under 4, the language milestones are the nearest guide: from 1 to 2 years children enjoy stories, songs and rhymes and point to named pictures in books, and at 2 years a typical child points to pictures in a book when asked. At 3 years a typical child draws a circle when shown how.
Some early precursors of reading trouble are described for the preschool years, such as being slow to talk or trouble learning rhymes, and these are covered in the next section.
Useful to find out: how language is developing, whether the child shows interest in books, songs and rhymes, and whether any close relatives had reading or learning difficulties.

Category: learning_academic | Age band: 4 to 5 years
Signs described for preschool-age children with possible dyslexia: mispronouncing familiar words, trouble finding the names of everyday objects and using vague words instead, trouble learning rhymes or song lyrics, trouble remembering sequences such as the alphabet, stories that are hard to follow or told out of order, and trouble remembering and following multi-step directions. Trouble learning letters, numbers, days of the week, colors and shapes and how to write one's name is also listed.
Signs described for possible math difficulty at this age: trouble learning to count, skipping numbers long after peers can say them in order, not seeming to understand what counting means, and trouble connecting a number to a group of things. Not understanding ideas like more and less is also listed.
Typical milestones: at 4 years a child says some words from a song, story or rhyme, names a few colors, draws a person with three or more body parts and holds a crayon between thumb and fingers rather than in a fist. At 5 years a child counts to 10, names some numbers from 1 to 5, uses time words like yesterday and tomorrow, writes some letters of their name, names some letters, and uses or recognizes simple rhymes.
Useful to find out: letters, sounds and name writing; rhymes and songs; counting; whether multi-step directions are followed; family history of reading or learning difficulty.

Category: learning_academic | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Kindergarten through grade 2, dyslexia signs: trouble learning letter names and their sounds, confusing letters that look or sound alike, struggling with familiar words especially without picture clues, substituting words when reading aloud, trouble separating and blending sounds in words, and trouble remembering spellings and spelling rules.
Grades 3 to 5: skipping or confusing small words when reading aloud, trouble sounding out new words, slow recognition of common words, trouble retelling a story or answering questions about details, repeated errors such as reversing letters, inconsistent spelling (the same word spelled correctly and incorrectly on one page) and avoiding reading or becoming frustrated by it.
Math: in grade school, trouble recalling basic math facts and using symbols like plus and minus correctly, still counting on fingers often, avoiding math or strategy games, and preferring to play alone rather than join card or board games.
Writing: trouble forming letters, closing them, keeping them the same size, spacing letters, getting tired when writing, and expressing ideas in writing. Messy slow handwriting and trouble lining up numbers are also linked to coordination difficulties.
Useful to find out: which skill is hard (reading, spelling, writing, math), how it looks (slow, inaccurate, avoided), what the teacher reports, how long homework takes, and whether the child says words aloud while writing or leaves words unfinished.

Category: learning_academic | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Signs described for tweens, teens and adults with dyslexia: slow reading, omitting small words and parts of longer words, trouble remembering abbreviations, frequent word-finding problems or using substitutes, trouble with jokes, idioms and puns, taking much longer than others to finish reading assignments, and understanding information better when it is read aloud.
For math, in high school the difficulties may start to show outside math class, for example when learning to drive. For writing and organization in middle and high school, trouble taking notes from slides, writing structured essays, managing long assignments and deadlines is described under visual-spatial and slow processing speed profiles.
The sources describe a long road to identification for some students, with some diagnosed only in middle or high school after years of feeling misunderstood, and emotional strain such as anxiety or low mood developing in students who feel constantly behind.
Useful to find out: what has been hard since elementary school, how reading and writing speed compare with classmates, whether extra time or reading aloud helps, avoidance of school tasks, and mood and confidence.

CATEGORY 3: SPEECH AND LANGUAGE (speech_language)
##########################################################################

Category: speech_language | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: how a child produces speech sounds, understands what others say (receptive language) and uses words and sentences to share thoughts (expressive language). A language disorder means trouble understanding others or sharing thoughts. A speech disorder means trouble producing speech sounds correctly, or hesitating or stuttering when talking. Developmental language disorder (DLD) is a language disorder that delays mastery of language skills, and some children with it do not begin to talk until their third or fourth year. Apraxia of speech is a speech disorder that makes it hard to put sounds and syllables in the correct order to form words.
How caregivers often describe it: "he isn't talking yet", "she only says a few words", "nobody can understand him", "she stutters", "he can't find the word he wants", "he doesn't follow directions", "she doesn't answer questions", "he mixes up words and sounds", "he repeats what I say", "she talks in short chopped sentences", "he doesn't seem to understand me".
Often overlaps with or is confused with: hearing loss (a hearing test is often part of a speech-language evaluation), autism, dyslexia (which is language-based), coordination problems affecting the movements for clear speech, and shyness or exposure to more than one language. Social use of language is covered under the social communication category.

Category: speech_language | Age band: under 4 years
Milestones by age from NIDCD: birth to 3 months reacts to loud sounds, calms or smiles when spoken to and coos. 4 to 6 months follows sounds with the eyes and babbles with sounds like p, b and m. 7 months to 1 year enjoys peek-a-boo, turns toward sounds, understands words for common items, uses gestures such as waving, imitates speech sounds and says one or two words by the first birthday. 1 to 2 years points to body parts when asked, follows simple commands, learns new words regularly and combines two words. 2 to 3 years has a word for nearly everything, uses two- or three-word phrases and is understood by family and friends. 3 to 4 years answers who, what, where and why questions, talks about daycare or preschool, uses sentences of four or more words and speaks without repeating syllables or words.
CDC checklists add: at 18 months says three or more words besides mama or dada and follows one-step directions without gestures. At 2 years points to pictures in a book when asked, says at least two words together, points to at least two body parts when asked and uses gestures beyond waving and pointing. At 30 months says about 50 words and uses two or more words together including an action word. At 3 years has back-and-forth conversation, asks who, what, where or why questions, says first name when asked and is understandable most of the time.
Delay in speech or language, or a no answer on the milestone checklist, is a reason to talk with a doctor. A delay can come from hearing loss or a speech or language disorder. A hearing test is often included in the evaluation. Early intervention programs for children under 3 and public schools for children 3 and older can evaluate without a referral.
Useful to find out: first words and when, how many words now, whether the child combines words, understands directions, points and gestures, responds to name, is understood by others, hearing checks, ear infections, and languages heard at home.

Category: speech_language | Age band: 4 to 5 years
Milestones at 4 to 5 years from NIDCD: follows a short story and answers simple questions about it, understands most of what is said at home and school, uses detailed sentences and stays on topic when telling stories, communicates easily with children and adults, says most sounds correctly (l, s, r, v, z, ch, sh and th may still be difficult), uses rhyming words and names some letters and numbers.
CDC checklists: at 4 years speaks in sentences of four or more words, says some words from a song, story or rhyme, talks about something that happened during the day and answers simple questions such as what a coat is for. At 5 years tells a story with at least two events, answers simple questions about a book, keeps a conversation going for more than three back-and-forth exchanges and uses or recognizes simple rhymes.
Signs sometimes seen with later reading trouble: mispronouncing familiar words, trouble finding the right word and trouble learning rhymes or song lyrics.
Useful to find out: whether others understand the child, sentence length, storytelling, following multi-step directions, sound errors and stuttering, and hearing checks.

Category: speech_language | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Sources give few age-specific milestones after age 5. Language disorders in school-age children are described as including word-finding difficulties (often using filler words while searching), slower naming, trouble with listening comprehension such as understanding questions and following directions, trouble with synonyms, and difficulty starting and keeping up conversations or adjusting a message to the listener. Language difficulties often show up as trouble with reading, writing and social communication in school.
Dyslexia is language-based, and signs include trouble finding names for objects, trouble retelling a story or answering questions about key details, and skipping or confusing small words when reading aloud.
Useful to find out: word-finding, understanding directions and questions, retelling events, conversation, and how reading, writing and friendships are affected.

Category: speech_language | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Sources give little teen-specific language information. Described in teens and tweens: frequent word-finding problems or using substitute words, trouble with jokes, idioms and puns, slow speech with vague placeholder words, trouble following fast conversations, and trouble understanding sarcasm. Some of these are described under dyslexia and slow processing speed.
Useful to find out: word-finding and understanding, conversation and sarcasm, and how reading, writing and school participation are affected.

CATEGORY 4: SOCIAL COMMUNICATION AND PEER RELATIONSHIPS (social_communication)
##########################################################################

Category: social_communication | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: how a child connects with other people: eye contact, responding to their name, facial expressions, gestures, sharing interests, pointing to show things, play with others, pretend play, noticing how others feel, making and keeping friends, understanding body language and tone, and following the unwritten rules of conversation. Social communication disorder is described as persistent difficulty using verbal and nonverbal language for social purposes, with trouble in social interaction, social understanding, pragmatics (using language in context) or language processing. Autism is described as problems with social communication and interaction together with restricted or repetitive behaviors or interests, and the repetitive behaviors are what set autism apart from conditions defined by social communication problems alone.
How caregivers often describe it: "he doesn't answer to his name", "she doesn't make eye contact", "he doesn't point or show me things", "she plays alone", "he doesn't play pretend", "he doesn't seem to notice other kids", "he has no friends", "she takes everything literally", "he doesn't get jokes or sarcasm", "he stands too close", "he talks only about his one topic", "he interrupts and changes the subject", "she doesn't notice when someone is upset".
Some people without autism also show some of these signs, and a child with autism may not show all of them. Often overlaps with or is confused with: language disorders, hearing problems, ADHD (trouble taking turns and getting along), anxiety (fear of places with people such as school), visual-spatial difficulties (missing nonverbal cues), slow processing speed (trouble following fast conversations) and low mood (withdrawing from friends).

Category: social_communication | Age band: under 4 years
CDC lists signs with ages: does not respond to name by 9 months, does not show facial expressions such as happy, sad, angry or surprised by 9 months, does not play simple interactive games like pat-a-cake by 12 months, uses few or no gestures such as waving by 12 months, does not share interests (such as showing an object they like) by 15 months, does not point to show something interesting by 18 months, does not notice when others are hurt or upset by 24 months, and does not notice other children or join them in play by 36 months. Avoiding eye contact is also listed.
Milestones: at 2 years a child notices when others are hurt or upset and looks at your face to see how to react in a new situation. At 30 months a child plays next to other children and sometimes with them, says "look at me", and follows simple routines such as cleaning up. At 3 years a child calms within about 10 minutes after a parent leaves and notices other children and joins them to play.
Autism begins before age 3. Some children show signs within the first 12 months, and in others signs do not show until 24 months or later. Some children gain skills until about 18 to 24 months and then stop gaining new skills or lose skills they had. Developmental and autism screening is recommended at set ages (autism screening at 18 and 24 months) and at any time there is a concern. Early intervention evaluation for children under 3 is free or low cost and needs no referral.
Useful to find out: when each sign was noticed, response to name, eye contact and smiling, pointing and showing, play with toys and people, interest in other children, and whether any skills have been lost.

Category: social_communication | Age band: 4 to 5 years
CDC lists signs: does not pretend to be someone else, such as a teacher or superhero, during play by 48 months, and does not sing, dance or act for others by 60 months.
Milestones: at 4 years a child pretends to be something else during play, asks to play with other children when none are around, comforts others who are hurt or sad, likes to help and changes behavior based on the place (such as a library or playground). At 5 years a child follows rules or takes turns in games with other children, sings, dances or acts for others and does simple chores.
Preschool-age children with NVLD-type profiles may prefer adults to peers and have trouble reading facial expressions or body language, and may prefer routine and become anxious with change.
Useful to find out: pretend play, play with other children, turn-taking, empathy, how the child behaves in groups, and how the child handles changes in routine.

Category: social_communication | Age band: 6 to 11 years
By school age children are generally expected to have mastered much of social communication: using language for different reasons (greeting, informing, requesting), changing language for the listener or setting (talking differently to a baby than to an adult, or on a playground than in a classroom), and following rules for conversation and storytelling such as taking turns and staying on topic. Some children struggle with school social interaction, which may be due to social communication disorder or autism.
Examples described for school-age children: difficulty making friends, interrupting or changing the topic abruptly, not picking up nonverbal cues, misreading classmates' intentions or feelings, trouble understanding nonliteral language and making inferences, answering before the other person has finished, and being teased. Missing jokes or sarcasm, taking language literally, struggling with tone, missing nonverbal cues, standing too close or too far and struggling to read the room are described in visual-spatial profiles. Younger children with mental health concerns may show little interest in playing with other children or trouble making friends. Children with coordination difficulties may have trouble making friends and be bullied. Trouble getting along with others and trouble taking turns are listed among ADHD signs.
Useful to find out: friendships and how they form and break, play preferences, conversation (turn-taking, topic, literal understanding), reading of cues, teasing or bullying, and what teachers see at recess and in groups.

Category: social_communication | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Adolescents and young adults with autism may have difficulties developing and maintaining friendships, communicating with peers and adults, and understanding what behaviors are expected in school or at work.
Described in older children and teens with visual-spatial or slow processing profiles: trouble detecting sarcasm, inside jokes, group cues and subtle exclusion, trouble following fast conversations or changes of topic, feeling overwhelmed in unstructured times, trouble keeping up with social media interactions, and avoiding groups or events such as dances. Avoiding groups may look like social anxiety but may stem from sensory or visual-spatial overload. Spending more and more time alone and avoiding friends or family is also listed among warning signs for mood problems in older children and teens.
Useful to find out: close friends, group participation, conversation pace and sarcasm, social media use, withdrawal and when it started, and mood.

CATEGORY 5: REPETITIVE BEHAVIORS, RESTRICTED INTERESTS AND TICS (repetitive_restricted)
##########################################################################

Category: repetitive_restricted | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: repeated movements, words or routines, narrow intense interests, strong need for sameness, and tics. CDC lists these restricted or repetitive behaviors and interests: lining up toys or objects and getting upset when the order changes, repeating words or phrases (echolalia), playing with toys the same way every time, focusing on parts of objects such as wheels, getting upset by minor changes, having obsessive interests, needing to follow certain routines, and hand-flapping, body-rocking or spinning. Unusual reactions to how things sound, smell, taste, look or feel are listed with these. Tics are described as sudden, brief, repeated twitches, movements or sounds that people cannot fully stop; motor tics involve the body (blinking, shoulder shrugging, facial grimacing, head jerking) and vocal tics involve sounds (throat clearing, sniffing, grunting). Simple tics involve a few muscle groups and complex tics are coordinated patterns of movement.
How caregivers often describe it: "he lines everything up", "she flaps her hands when excited", "he spins or rocks", "he repeats the same line from a show", "he's obsessed with one thing", "he plays with toys the same way every time", "she melts down if the routine changes", "he keeps blinking", "she clears her throat all the time", "he makes noises he can't stop", "she checks things again and again".
Having tics is described as a little like having hiccups: a person can sometimes hold a tic back for a while, but it is hard. Tics come and go, change over time and can disappear for weeks or months. Tourette syndrome means both motor and vocal tics for at least a year. About 1 in 50 children aged 5 to 14 is described as having Tourette syndrome or a persistent tic disorder.
Often overlaps with or is confused with: autism, sensory processing differences, anxiety and checking behaviors, ADHD, and stress. Repeating actions or checking things many times out of fear that something bad will happen is listed among warning signs for younger children. Repeated hand movements such as wringing, clapping or mouthing are also described in Rett syndrome after loss of hand skills, so a new onset together with lost skills belongs with the skill loss category.

Category: repetitive_restricted | Age band: under 4 years
In toddlers, repetitive behaviors can include hand flapping, spinning, holding a particular posture, an unusual way of playing with toys such as holding them instead of playing with them, a fixation on one interest such as water, and strong reactions to loud noises or scratchy textures. CDC lists the repetitive behaviors without ages.
Tics usually start later: first tic symptoms are usually between ages 5 and 10.
Useful to find out: what the behavior looks like, how often, whether the child can be interrupted, whether it is linked to excitement or distress, play style, reactions to change, and when it started.

Category: repetitive_restricted | Age band: 4 to 5 years
The same repetitive behaviors and interests are described for this age range without separate age detail. Play that is the same every time, lining up, distress when order or routine changes, and intense interests are the examples.
Tics usually begin between ages 5 and 10 and often start in the head and neck, such as blinking or facial grimaces.
Useful to find out: the specific movements or sounds, whether they come and go, how the child reacts to changes in routine, and whether the child can be flexible about play.

Category: repetitive_restricted | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Tics most often appear between ages 5 and 10, usually in the head and neck first, and may spread to the trunk, arms and legs. Motor tics usually come before vocal tics, and simple tics often come before complex tics. Tics vary in type, frequency, location and severity and can disappear for a while. A health professional looks at the type of tic and how long it has lasted.
Younger children with mental health concerns may repeat actions or check things many times out of fear that something bad will happen.
Useful to find out: which tics, how long, whether they change or move, whether they get worse with stress or tiredness, effects on school and friends, and teasing.

Category: repetitive_restricted | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Most people with Tourette syndrome have their worst tic symptoms in their early teens, and tics typically lessen and become more controlled as teens grow older. CDC lists repetitive behaviors for autism without teen-specific detail.
Useful to find out: tics, whether they are getting better or worse, effects on school and friends, stress and mood, and rituals or checking.

CATEGORY 6: SENSORY PROCESSING (sensory_processing)
##########################################################################

Category: sensory_processing | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: unusual reactions to sights, sounds, smells, tastes, touch and movement. Sensory processing issues are described as trouble organizing and responding to information that comes in through the senses. Children may be oversensitive (hypersensitive), becoming overwhelmed and avoiding sensory input; undersensitive (hyposensitive), seeking more sensory input and showing little reaction to heat, cold or pain; or a mix, oversensitive in some areas and undersensitive in others. Sensory seekers may give tight hugs, crash into things to feel pressure, touch people and objects often or make loud noises. Sensory avoiders may become overwhelmed and hyperactive.
How caregivers often describe it: "he covers his ears at the vacuum", "she can't stand tags and seams", "he gags on certain textures", "he's so picky about food", "he melts down in the supermarket", "bright lights bother her", "he crashes into things and people", "he doesn't seem to feel pain", "she's always touching things", "he plays too rough", "she hates getting her hair brushed or nails cut".
Signs listed with sensory challenges include playing roughly and taking physical risks, a high pain tolerance, squirming and fidgeting, being constantly on the move, invading personal space, being distracted or anxious and being clumsy and uncoordinated. Children with sensory processing differences may also have trouble with motor skills, balance and eye-hand coordination.
Often overlaps with or is confused with: autism (unusual reactions to sound, smell, taste, look or feel are listed among autism signs), ADHD (fidgeting, constant motion, distraction), anxiety, coordination difficulties and food refusal. Hearing and vision checks are worth doing when a child seems slow to respond.
The sources used here do not give detailed age-by-age sensory signs. They describe a typical pattern in which sensitivity to noise, light, clothing and food appears in the preschool years, followed by a teacher noticing clumsiness, fine motor trouble and meltdowns.

Category: sensory_processing | Age band: under 4 years
Described in toddlers and preschoolers: unusual sensitivity to noise or light and being very picky about clothes and shoes ("too scratchy", "too tight"). In toddlers, repetitive behaviors may include strong reactions to loud noises or scratchy textures. Autism signs include unusual reactions to how things sound, smell, taste, look or feel.
Useful to find out: which senses are involved (sound, touch, taste, light, movement), how strong the reaction is, how often, whether it is the same in different places, and what helps.

Category: sensory_processing | Age band: 4 to 5 years
The same patterns are described for preschool and early school age: strong dislike of certain clothes and food textures, trouble with messy play, seeking intense movement and pressure, and meltdowns around dressing, eating and transitions. A teacher may notice clumsiness and trouble holding a pencil.
Useful to find out: the pattern across home, preschool and outings, effect on eating, dressing, sleep and play, and fine motor skills.

Category: sensory_processing | Age band: 6 to 11 years
In kids with sensory processing issues, outbursts or upset in response to everyday settings (a supermarket, new shoes) are often about sensory input and not defiance. Clumsiness, fidgeting and constant motion, personal space problems and anxiety are described.
Useful to find out: situations that overwhelm, the effect on school (noise, lights, seating), eating and clothing choices, and coordination.

Category: sensory_processing | Age band: 12 to 17 years
The sources used give no teen-specific sensory information beyond general descriptions of oversensitivity and undersensitivity. Visual sensitivity such as discomfort with bright or fluorescent lights and cluttered environments, and difficulty with noise in classrooms are described for neurodivergent children generally, and avoidance of crowded events can come from sensory overload instead of social anxiety.
Useful to find out: situations that overwhelm, what the teen avoids, school settings, and mood.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 7: MOTOR COORDINATION (motor_coordination)
##########################################################################

Category: motor_coordination | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: large movements such as running, jumping, balance and ball skills (gross motor), small movements such as buttoning, using utensils, cutting and handwriting (fine motor), and overall coordination. Developmental coordination disorder (DCD, also called dyspraxia) is described as a condition in which a child performs less well than expected for their age in daily activities and moves clumsily. Clumsiness alone does not mean DCD, since many clumsy children have typical motor skills. DCD often occurs with ADHD, dyslexia and autism, and some children have trouble coordinating the movements needed for clear speech. A definite diagnosis usually does not happen until a child is about 5 or older because children vary widely in their rate of development.
How caregivers often describe it: "he's so clumsy", "she trips and falls all the time", "he can't ride a bike", "she can't catch a ball", "he can't tie his shoes or do buttons", "his handwriting is a scribble", "she avoids sports and PE", "he can't use scissors", "he was late to walk", "he bumps into things and drops things".
Often overlaps with or is confused with: sensory processing differences, ADHD, dysgraphia, visual-spatial difficulties, and, when skills are lost or movement is getting worse, the skill loss category. New weakness, loss of movement or unusual movements call for medical review.

Category: motor_coordination | Age band: under 4 years
CDC milestones: at 18 months walks without holding on, scribbles, drinks from a cup without a lid, feeds self with fingers, tries to use a spoon and climbs on and off a couch without help. At 2 years kicks a ball, runs, walks up a few stairs with or without help and eats with a spoon. At 30 months uses hands to twist things like doorknobs or lids, takes off some clothes, jumps with both feet and turns book pages one at a time. At 3 years strings large beads, puts on some clothes such as loose pants or a jacket and uses a fork.
In infants with DCD, rolling over, sitting, crawling or walking may come slightly later than expected, unusual body positions may appear in the first year, toys needing good coordination such as stacking bricks may be hard, and learning to eat with cutlery may be difficult. These signs may come and go. Children with visual-spatial difficulties may show clumsiness, awkward use of crayons or scissors, trouble buttoning and trouble learning to ride a tricycle.
Useful to find out: when the child sat, crawled and walked, how they manage stairs, cups, spoons and play with blocks, and whether skills have been lost.

Category: motor_coordination | Age band: 4 to 5 years
CDC milestones: at 4 years catches a large ball most of the time, serves food or pours water with supervision, unbuttons some buttons and holds a crayon or pencil between fingers and thumb. At 5 years buttons some buttons and hops on one foot. Throwing and catching a ball and holding and using utensils are among the early difficulties described for preschool-age children with DCD.
Useful to find out: ball skills, hopping and balance, pencil grip, buttons and dressing, and whether the child avoids playground and motor games.

Category: motor_coordination | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Described in school-age children with DCD: difficulty with hopping, jumping, running, catching and kicking, avoiding games and finding PE hard, trouble with stairs, scribbled or underdeveloped handwriting and drawings, trouble getting dressed, doing up buttons and tying shoelaces, restless movements such as swinging arms and legs, bumping into things, dropping objects and falling often, and poor sports performance leading to less exercise. Other difficulties listed include a short attention span, trouble following instructions or copying information, poor organization, slow skill learning, trouble making friends, possible bullying, frustration-related behavior problems and low self-esteem.
Handwriting problems also appear with dysgraphia: trouble forming letters, spacing, and tiring when writing.
Useful to find out: which tasks are hardest (writing, sports, dressing), effects on school and friends, therapy history, and hand dominance.

Category: motor_coordination | Age band: 12 to 17 years
The sources do not describe teen-specific coordination signs, only that some problems may become obvious only as children get older. In visual-spatial profiles, trouble finding routes and learning to drive are described in high school.
Useful to find out: ongoing handwriting and sports difficulties, driving readiness, and effects on confidence and participation.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 8: ADAPTIVE AND DAILY LIVING SKILLS (adaptive_daily_living)
##########################################################################

Category: adaptive_daily_living | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: the everyday skills a child uses to manage daily life and gain independence: feeding, dressing, toileting, hygiene, following routines, helping with chores, packing a bag, finding the way and keeping safe. These skills build step by step and depend on caregiver guidance and the child's experiences. Some children learn faster than others. Some children have a disability or motor difficulty that makes self-care harder, and cultural factors can influence the rate at which skills are expected to develop.
How caregivers often describe it: "she still needs help getting dressed", "he's not toilet trained", "she can't feed herself neatly", "he can't pack his own bag", "she needs to be reminded to do everything", "he can't get ready in the morning", "he doesn't help with chores", "he has no sense of danger", "she can't be left alone".
Often overlaps with or is confused with: motor coordination difficulties, global developmental delay, slow processing speed (everything takes a long time, such as getting out the door), executive function and ADHD (forgetting routines), and sensory issues (clothing, food, hygiene). A child who is not yet doing one self-care task may be perfectly typical. Several skills lagging behind, or progress that has stalled or reversed, is the pattern that matters, and lost skills belong with the skill loss category.

Category: adaptive_daily_living | Age band: under 4 years
CDC milestones: at 18 months feeds self with fingers, tries to use a spoon, helps dress by pushing an arm through a sleeve, and copies chores such as sweeping. At 2 years eats with a spoon. At 30 months takes off some clothes and follows simple routines such as cleaning up. At 3 years puts on some clothes alone and uses a fork, and avoids touching hot objects when warned.
Useful to find out: feeding, dressing, toileting progress, following routines, safety awareness, and whether any skills have been lost.

Category: adaptive_daily_living | Age band: 4 to 5 years
CDC milestones: at 4 years serves food or pours water with supervision, unbuttons some buttons, avoids dangers such as jumping from tall heights, likes to help and changes behavior based on the place. At 5 years buttons some buttons and does simple chores such as matching socks or clearing the table. Around ages 3 to 4 most self-care skills begin to emerge, though mastery may not come before age 6, and children with developmental delays may take longer. A practice-based chart lists dressing independently and having a morning routine around ages 5 to 6.
Useful to find out: dressing, toileting, eating with utensils, chores, safety, and how many skills lag.

Category: adaptive_daily_living | Age band: 6 to 11 years
One practice-based chart lists independent daytime and night toileting around 6 to 7 years, feeding self without difficulty, coping in busy or noisy places and telling time, and around 7 to 8 years opening lunch boxes and packing a school bag with little prompting. Difficulty with self-care tasks such as dressing, feeding and toileting at these ages is described alongside poor organization and delayed fine motor skills. Slow processing speed can show up as being unable to finish homework in a reasonable time or getting out the door in the morning.
Useful to find out: self-care and independence compared with peers, morning and bedtime routines, organization of belongings, and what the child can do with prompting versus alone.

Category: adaptive_daily_living | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Teens are expected to organize their own work, keep track of assignments and deadlines and monitor their own progress. Described in visual-spatial and slow processing profiles: difficulty managing long-term projects, estimating time, route-finding, remembering materials, poor time management and being late on assignments. The sources used give little detail on self-care skills for teens.
Useful to find out: independence with schoolwork, time management, getting around, and daily routines.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 9: MEMORY AND INFORMATION PROCESSING (memory_processing)
##########################################################################

Category: memory_processing | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: how well a child holds information in mind, follows instructions, and how quickly the child takes in information, makes sense of it and starts to respond. Working memory is the ability to hold information in mind and use it. Processing speed is described as how quickly a person takes in information, makes sense of it and begins to respond. The information can be spoken, visual (letters and numbers) or related to movement. Slow processing speed has nothing to do with intelligence, and a child may be slow at one task and quick at another. When neuropsychologists use the term they usually mean slow performance on the processing speed subtests of a standard intelligence test for children. A related pattern called sluggish cognitive tempo is described as seeming sleepy or tired, daydreaming and moving slowly, and many but not all of these children also show slow processing speed.
How caregivers often describe it: "he forgets what I just told him", "she can't follow more than one instruction", "he takes forever to answer", "she's slow to finish everything", "he loses his place", "he can't keep up in class", "he stares off into space", "he needs everything repeated", "he can't get out the door in the morning".
Often overlaps with or is confused with: ADHD (forgetting daily obligations, seeming not to listen), dyslexia (trouble with multi-step directions and sequences), language disorders (listening comprehension), anxiety and depression (trouble paying attention), insufficient sleep (sleeping the recommended hours is linked to better attention, learning and memory), hearing and vision problems, and visual-spatial difficulties. A change in memory or speed over time can have medical causes and belongs with the skill loss category.

Category: memory_processing | Age band: under 4 years
The sources give no processing-speed or memory signs specifically for under 4. Milestones give the nearest guide: at 18 months a typical child follows one-step directions without gestures, and at 30 months follows two-step instructions. At 18 to 36 months children are developing the ability to keep attention on a goal and try new strategies.
Useful to find out: how many steps in an instruction the child follows, how quickly they respond, and whether this is new or has always been so.

Category: memory_processing | Age band: 4 to 5 years
Signs described for preschool-age children with slow processing speed: trouble following directions with multiple steps, taking a long time to write their name, struggling to answer questions especially over the phone, often seeming zoned out during group time, taking longer than peers to learn to use paper and pencil, and being slow with routines such as putting on a coat, which can make them late in the morning. Trouble remembering and following multi-step directions is also listed among early signs of dyslexia.
Useful to find out: instruction length that works, pace of routines, time to answer, and where the child seems to lose track.

Category: memory_processing | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Signs described for grade-school children: taking a very long time to copy notes in class, being unable to finish homework in a reasonable time, trouble understanding busy settings such as the playground where many things happen at once, difficulty making quick decisions, struggling with timed tests and then rushing and making careless errors, and trouble following conversations and answering when friends ask questions. After reading a paragraph, a child with poor working memory may forget what was read, and may stop an assignment because they forgot the directions.
Useful to find out: what is forgotten and how soon, homework time, timed tasks, and whether it is the same in all subjects and settings.

Category: memory_processing | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Signs described for middle school: trouble taking notes while the teacher is speaking, trouble following fast conversations in person or online and missing sarcasm and social cues, feeling overwhelmed when given a lot of information at once, often not finishing timed tests (especially multi-step math), needing more time to answer and decide, slow speech with trouble finding specific words, and trouble finishing projects on time. For high school: trouble keeping up with lectures and taking notes, rarely joining class discussions, missing nuances when friends change topic, difficulty in classes needing quick visual understanding, telling stories so slowly they lose track, poor time management, being late on assignments and trouble with long-term assignments and keeping track of details.
Useful to find out: note-taking and lectures, test timing, deadlines, and discussion participation.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 10: EMOTIONAL REGULATION, ANXIETY AND MOOD (emotional_mood)
##########################################################################

Category: emotional_mood | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: worry, fear, sadness, irritability, mood swings and trouble managing feelings. Because these problems mostly involve thoughts and feelings they are sometimes called internalizing problems. Anxiety types named: separation anxiety (very afraid when away from parents), phobias, social anxiety (fear of places with people, such as school), generalized anxiety and panic disorder (pounding heart, trouble breathing, dizziness, shaking or sweating). Anxiety in children can also look like irritability or anger, trouble sleeping, tiredness, headaches and stomachaches, and anxious children may keep worries to themselves so they are missed. Depression signs: persistent sadness, hopelessness or irritability, losing interest in activities, changes in appetite or sleep, low energy or restlessness, trouble paying attention, feeling worthless or guilty, self-injury and in extreme cases thoughts of suicide. Some depressed children do not look sad and show misbehavior, low motivation or apparent laziness that others misread.
How caregivers often describe it: "she worries about everything", "he cries and clings when I leave", "she won't go to school", "he's afraid of everything", "she has stomachaches before school", "he's irritable all the time", "she's sad and says she's no good", "he lost interest in everything he loved", "she has big mood swings", "he has meltdowns", "she won't talk to anyone".
Fears and worries are typical in children, for example toddlers often become very distressed when away from parents even when safe. It becomes a concern when fear or sadness is persistent or extreme and interferes with school, home or play. NIMH advises considering help when behaviors or emotions last for weeks or longer, cause distress to the child or family, or interfere with functioning. Seek help immediately if a child's behavior is unsafe or the child talks about hurting themselves or someone else; in the US call or text 988 for suicidal thoughts or crisis, or call 911 in life-threatening situations.
Depression and anxiety often occur together and often occur with ADHD, autism, learning problems and disruptive behavior. Trauma can cause similar signs. Insufficient sleep is linked to depression and, in teenagers, to higher risk of self-harm and suicidal thoughts.

Category: emotional_mood | Age band: under 4 years
The sources give little mood-specific detail for under 4. Toddlers often become very distressed when away from their parents even if safe and cared for, which is typical. Typical milestones: at 2 years a child notices when others are hurt or upset, and at 3 years calms down within about 10 minutes after a parent leaves, such as at child care drop-off.
Useful to find out: how the child reacts to separation, how long distress lasts, whether it affects daily life, and sleep and eating.

Category: emotional_mood | Age band: 4 to 5 years
Warning signs listed for younger children (no age cutoff is given): frequent tantrums or irritability much of the time, often seeming fearful or worried, frequent stomachaches or headaches with no known medical cause, sleeping too much or too little, frequent nightmares or daytime sleepiness, and little interest in playing with other children. Nighttime fears are commonly reported in this age range.
Useful to find out: the main worry or mood, how long, how often, physical complaints, sleep, and effects on preschool and play.

Category: emotional_mood | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Warning signs listed for younger children also include academic struggles or a recent drop in grades and repeating actions or checking things many times out of fear that something bad will happen. The CDC page gives age 8 and older as the range for anxiety screening.
Useful to find out: worries and fears, school avoidance, stomachaches and headaches, mood and irritability, interest in usual activities, friendships and sleep.

Category: emotional_mood | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Warning signs listed for older children and teens: lost interest in things they used to enjoy, low energy, sleeping too much or too little or being sleepy all day, periods of highly elevated energy and activity with much less sleep than usual, spending more time alone and avoiding friends or family, excessive dieting or exercise or fear of gaining weight, self-harm such as cutting or burning, smoking, drinking or drug use, risky or destructive behavior, thoughts of suicide, and believing someone is controlling their mind or hearing things others cannot. The CDC page gives age 12 and older as the range for depression screening. Many teens feel anxious about school or friendships at times, and sadness after losing someone close can be expected; persistence and interference are what matter.
Useful to find out: mood and interest, sleep and energy, withdrawal, safety, substance use, eating and exercise, and school functioning.
##########################################################################
CATEGORY 11: BEHAVIORAL REGULATION (behavioral_regulation)
##########################################################################

Category: behavioral_regulation | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: tantrums, defiance, arguing, aggression, rule-breaking and impulsive or unsafe behavior. Oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) is described as an ongoing pattern of uncooperative, defiant and hostile behavior toward authority figures that seriously interferes with daily functioning. Listed behaviors: frequent temper tantrums, excessive arguing with adults, often questioning rules, active defiance and refusing requests, deliberately annoying or upsetting people, blaming others for mistakes, being easily annoyed or touchy, frequent anger and resentment, mean or hateful speech when upset, and spitefulness. Symptoms usually show in several settings but may be more noticeable at home or at school. Conduct disorder is described as repetitive, persistent patterns including aggression to people and animals, destroying property, deceitfulness, lying or stealing, and serious rule violations such as staying out at night, running away and skipping school.
How caregivers often describe it: "he has constant tantrums", "she argues about everything", "he refuses to do anything I ask", "he hits and bites", "she's aggressive with other kids", "he lies and steals", "she breaks things", "he runs away", "he can't control himself", "he acts without thinking".
All children are oppositional at times, especially when tired, hungry, stressed or upset, and oppositional behavior is a normal part of development for two- to three-year-olds and early adolescents. It becomes a concern when frequent and consistent enough to stand out from children of the same age and developmental level and when it affects social, family or school life. There is no specific time threshold given in the family guide.
Often overlaps with or is confused with: ADHD (impulsivity, trouble taking turns), learning disabilities, mood disorders (depression can show up as misbehavior), anxiety, trauma (abuse, neglect and traumatic experiences are listed as risk factors for conduct problems and can cause similar signs), insufficient sleep (linked to attention and behavior problems), frustration from coordination or language difficulties, and sensory overload. A child with these behaviors should have a comprehensive evaluation that looks for coexisting conditions, since symptoms may not improve unless those are addressed too.

Category: behavioral_regulation | Age band: under 4 years
Oppositional behavior is a normal part of development for two- to three-year-olds. For children under 4 with substantial impairment from behavior problems, the ADHD guideline says referral for parent training in behavior management can be considered. Children under 3 can be evaluated through early intervention without a referral.
Useful to find out: what the behaviors are, how often and how long they last, where they happen, what happens right before, safety concerns, and sleep.

Category: behavioral_regulation | Age band: 4 to 5 years
Frequent tantrums or irritability much of the time is listed among warning signs for younger children, along with being in constant motion and unable to sit quietly except in enjoyable activities. Typical milestone: at 5 years, a child follows rules or takes turns in games with other children.
Useful to find out: frequency, settings, triggers, aggression toward others, impact on preschool, and sleep.

Category: behavioral_regulation | Age band: 6 to 11 years
The ODD and conduct patterns above are described for children generally. ADHD signs include trouble taking turns, trouble resisting temptation, careless mistakes and unnecessary risks. Children with coordination difficulties may show behavior problems from frustration. Poor sleep is linked to behavior problems.
Useful to find out: which behaviors, which settings (home, school, peers), what happens before and after, aggression, lying or stealing, firesetting or cruelty to animals, and safety.

Category: behavioral_regulation | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Oppositional behavior is normal in early adolescence but a concern when frequent, consistent and impairing. Conduct problems can include staying out at night despite rules, running away and frequently skipping school. Risky or destructive behavior alone or with friends, and smoking, drinking or drug use, are listed among warning signs in older children and teens. Teens with ADHD are more likely to take risks including substance use.
Useful to find out: specific behaviors, school attendance, substance use, safety, peer group, and trauma or major stress.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 12: SLEEP (sleep)
##########################################################################

Category: sleep | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: how long a child sleeps, trouble falling asleep, trouble staying asleep, bedtime refusal, nightmares, sleepwalking, daytime sleepiness and sleep schedule. Recommended sleep per 24 hours (American Academy of Sleep Medicine consensus): infants 4 to 12 months 12 to 16 hours including naps; 1 to 2 years 11 to 14 hours including naps; 3 to 5 years 10 to 13 hours including naps; 6 to 12 years 9 to 12 hours; 13 to 18 years 8 to 10 hours. Sleeping less than recommended is linked to problems with attention, behavior and learning, and with accidents, injuries, high blood pressure, obesity, diabetes and depression; in teenagers it is linked to higher risk of self-harm, suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts.
Behavioral insomnia of childhood is described as bedtime resistance (crying, protests, repeated requests, refusing to get into or stay in bed), long time to fall asleep and night wakings. Some is due to limit-setting problems and some to sleep-onset associations such as needing feeding, rocking, lights, TV, music or a caregiver nearby. It is most common up to age 5 but can continue. Insomnia is described as affecting 20 to 30 percent of children.
How caregivers often describe it: "she won't go to bed", "it takes hours for him to fall asleep", "he wakes up all night", "she has nightmares", "he sleepwalks", "she's exhausted all day", "he's up until 2 in the morning", "she can't wake up for school", "he needs me in the room to sleep".
Often overlaps with or is confused with: ADHD (sleep disorders and sleep apnea can look like or accompany ADHD), anxiety and depression (trouble sleeping, sleeping much more or less), autism (unusual sleeping habits are listed among common related characteristics), and behavior problems from tiredness.

Category: sleep | Age band: under 4 years
Recommended: 11 to 14 hours including naps at 1 to 2 years and 10 to 13 hours at 3 to 5 years. Bedtime settling difficulties and frequent night waking predominate in infancy and early childhood, and many young children repeatedly resist bedtime or wake repeatedly and demand parents' attention. Behavioral insomnia is most common in children up to 5 years old. In children with neurodevelopmental conditions, under-fives had the most night wakings and younger children had more bedtime resistance than older children.
Useful to find out: bedtime, wake time and naps, how long to fall asleep, night wakings, what helps the child sleep (feeding, rocking, a caregiver nearby), and how the child behaves the next day.

Category: sleep | Age band: 4 to 5 years
Recommended 10 to 13 hours including naps. Bedtime resistance, delayed sleep onset, sleep-related anxiety, night waking and parasomnias are more frequent in younger children than older children. Nighttime fears are commonly reported by children in this range. Nightmares are described for this age range among warning signs for younger children.
Useful to find out: total sleep, bedtime resistance, fears, nightmares, night wakings, and daytime sleepiness or irritability.

Category: sleep | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Recommended 9 to 12 hours. Difficulty falling asleep and night waking affect 15 to 30 percent of children aged 3 to 12. Nighttime fears are very commonly reported at ages 7 to 12. Nightmares and sleepwalking feature more in older children than in toddlers.
Useful to find out: total sleep on school nights, bedtime routine, screens at bedtime, fears and nightmares, sleepwalking, and effect on attention and behavior at school.

Category: sleep | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Recommended 8 to 10 hours. Poor sleep habits and circadian rhythm problems (such as a delayed sleep phase, being unable to fall asleep until late) become more prominent in adolescence, and daytime sleepiness is more common in older adolescents. Insomnia in adolescents may show as bedtime resistance, hyperactivity, academic difficulties and other behavior problems. Sleeping less than recommended is linked to depression and, in teens, to higher risk of self-harm and suicidal thoughts.
Useful to find out: bedtime and wake time on school days and weekends, time to fall asleep, daytime sleepiness, mood, and effect on school.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 13: SKILL LOSS OR REGRESSION (skill_regression)
##########################################################################

Category: skill_regression | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: a child who has lost skills they previously had, as opposed to a child who is slow to gain new skills. Examples across the sources: lost words or language, lost gestures or play, lost social interaction, lost hand skills, lost ability to crawl or walk. Any loss or regression of a previously acquired skill at any age is described as an indication for a complete diagnostic evaluation, including neurological, metabolic and genetic evaluation as appropriate. CDC milestone guidance tells caregivers to act early if a child is not meeting milestones or has lost skills they once had, and not to wait.
How caregivers often describe it: "he used to say words and now he doesn't", "she stopped talking", "he used to wave and make eye contact and stopped", "she can't do what she used to do", "he lost skills", "he's gone backward", "she stopped playing the way she did", "he stopped walking or using his hands".
Distinguish loss from slowing: in studies of autism, an initial yes to a question about regression has sometimes changed to no after further questions when the child had failed to gain expected new skills but had not actually lost any. Asking for a specific example of what was lost, and when, helps.
Patterns in the sources: in autism, some children gain skills until about 18 to 24 months and then stop gaining or lose skills, and a "regressive" onset describes typical development for the first year or two followed by loss of skills in the second year along with autism signs. In Rett syndrome, development appears normal at first, then typically between 6 and 18 months stalls and is followed by loss of social engagement, language, purposeful hand use and the ability to crawl or walk, either suddenly or over weeks to months, followed by repetitive hand movements. Childhood disintegrative disorder is described as very rare, with loss of language and social skills after normal development for the first years (a reported case describes onset after age 5), with abnormal brain wave tests in about half of cases and seizures sometimes. Case reports also describe late-onset regression with psychiatric features that overlaps with these conditions and with childhood-onset schizophrenia.
These sources describe causes and patterns; they do not tell a caregiver what is happening in a particular child. Loss of skills calls for prompt review by a doctor.

Category: skill_regression | Age band: under 4 years
CDC says some children with autism gain skills and meet milestones until about 18 to 24 months and then stop gaining new skills or lose skills. Rett syndrome regression typically begins between 6 and 18 months. The CDC milestone booklet says to act early if a child has lost skills they once had. Early intervention evaluation for children under 3 is free or low cost and needs no referral.
Useful to find out: exactly what was lost (words, gestures, eye contact, play, walking or hand use), the age it was noticed, how quickly it happened (suddenly or over weeks or months), whether other changes came with it, whether the child has had a medical evaluation for it, and whether any skills are coming back.

Category: skill_regression | Age band: 4 to 5 years
Any loss of a previously acquired skill at any age warrants complete evaluation. Childhood disintegrative disorder is described as a rare condition with loss of language and social skills after normal early development. CDC milestone guidance applies: act early if a child has lost skills.
Useful to find out: what was lost, when, how fast, anything unusual around that time, and whether the child is losing more skills.

Category: skill_regression | Age band: 6 to 11 years
The sources used give few school-age-specific details. The general statement applies: loss of a previously acquired skill at any age warrants a complete evaluation. A reported case of childhood disintegrative disorder describes a 10-year-old who developed normally until age 5 and then lost language and social skills and developed repetitive hand movements. Younger children with mental health concerns may show a recent drop in grades; that is listed as a mood or learning warning sign and not as skill loss by itself.
Useful to find out: what the child could do before and cannot do now (speech, handwriting, walking, school skills), when it began, how fast, and whether other changes came with it.

Category: skill_regression | Age band: 12 to 17 years
The sources used give no teen-specific regression information beyond the general statement that loss of previously acquired skills at any age warrants a complete evaluation, and case reports of late-onset regression with psychiatric features. Loss of interest in activities, withdrawal, and low energy are listed under mood warning signs for older children and teens.
Useful to find out: what changed and when, how fast, mood and sleep, substance use, injury or illness, and school functioning.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 14: GLOBAL DEVELOPMENTAL DELAY (global_delay)
##########################################################################

Category: global_delay | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: slower development across several areas at once. Global developmental delay (GDD) is defined as significant delay in two or more developmental domains (gross and fine motor, speech and language, cognition, social and personal skills, and activities of daily living) in children under 5 years. Significant delay is often defined as performance at least two standard deviations below average on standardized tests, or functional age well below chronological age, and delay is graded as mild, moderate or severe. Developmental delay is not a diagnosis by itself. The term is reserved for children under 5 and signals something that could be transient, although in practice many children continue to show differences into later childhood. Developmental delays occur in about 10 to 15 percent of preschool children and global delays in about 1 to 3 percent. For older children, intellectual disability, defined as significant limits in both intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior beginning before age 18, is the usual term when IQ testing is valid. A child born more than 3 weeks early should be judged by corrected age.
How caregivers often describe it: "she's behind in everything", "he's late for talking and walking", "he's like a much younger child", "she isn't meeting milestones", "the doctor said global delay", "he can't do what other kids his age do".
Evaluation described: general and systemic exam, growth measures, hearing and vision assessment, blood tests if needed and referral to a developmental pediatrician. Causes are varied: up to about 40 percent of cases may be related to genetic factors, with others metabolic, prenatal or perinatal, or related to social and environmental factors. Early intervention improves outcomes.
Often overlaps with or is confused with: delay in only one area (such as isolated speech delay), autism, hearing or vision problems, prematurity (use corrected age) and under-stimulation or neglect.

Category: global_delay | Age band: under 4 years
CDC milestone checklists for 18 months, 2 years, 30 months and 3 years cover social and emotional, language and communication, cognitive and movement skills (see the individual categories for details). Developmental screening is recommended at set ages, and autism screening at 18 and 24 months, and at any time there is a concern. The checklist guidance says use the corrected age if the child was born more than 3 weeks early. Early intervention programs evaluate children under 3 at no or low cost without a referral.
Useful to find out: which areas are behind (talking, walking, play, self-care), by how much, whether the child was born early, whether skills are progressing slowly or have been lost, and hearing and vision checks.

Category: global_delay | Age band: 4 to 5 years
The term GDD is used until the child's fifth birthday. CDC milestone checklists at 4 and 5 years cover social, language, cognitive and movement skills. For children 3 and older, the public school system can provide a free evaluation and the person in charge of special education can be contacted without a referral.
Useful to find out: which developmental areas are behind, the pattern over time, services already received, and previous evaluations.

Category: global_delay | Age band: 6 to 11 years
The term GDD is not used after age 5. When delays continue, intellectual disability is considered; it is defined by limits in both intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior. The sources used give few other details for this age.
Useful to find out: earlier evaluations and diagnoses, school supports, learning pace and daily living skills.

Category: global_delay | Age band: 12 to 17 years
The sources used give no teen-specific information on global delay. See the 6 to 11 band for the term and the adaptive daily living category for daily skills.
Useful to find out: earlier evaluations and diagnoses, school supports, independence skills and transition planning.

##########################################################################
CATEGORY 15: VISUAL-SPATIAL AND NONVERBAL PROCESSING (visuospatial_nonverbal)
##########################################################################

Category: visuospatial_nonverbal | Section: overview | Age band: all ages
What it covers: how a child interprets and uses visual and spatial information. Visual-spatial processing is the ability to integrate and mentally manipulate visual information. It supports recognizing patterns, interpreting diagrams, organizing ideas and physical materials such as a backpack, finding the way around spaces, coordinating movement, and abilities such as reading an analog clock. Nonverbal learning disorder (NVLD) is described as trouble with visual-spatial processing; the name can mislead because children with NVLD have typical or often strong verbal ability. Some experts propose renaming it developmental visual-spatial disorder. NVLD is not in the DSM-5. Which visual-spatial skills are affected varies a lot from child to child.
How caregivers often describe it: "she's great at talking but terrible at puzzles", "he can't draw shapes or copy from the board", "he gets lost even in familiar places", "she can't read a clock", "his math is messy and he can't line up numbers", "he's clumsy", "he doesn't read faces", "he can't organize his backpack", "he has trouble with maps and diagrams", "he doesn't get jokes".
The signs often go unnoticed in early childhood because strong verbal skills hide visual-spatial weaknesses, and many children are not identified until middle or high school. The article lists overlap with other neurodevelopmental conditions, so the signs are not exclusive to NVLD. A comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation is recommended, and it helps to tell the evaluator about visual-spatial concerns beforehand.
Often overlaps with or is confused with: motor coordination difficulties, dyscalculia and math difficulty, dysgraphia, ADHD and executive function problems, social communication difficulties, and anxiety.

Category: visuospatial_nonverbal | Age band: under 4 years
Described for toddlers and preschoolers together: clumsiness, awkward use of crayons or scissors, trouble buttoning, difficulty learning to ride a tricycle, a gap between motor and language development as an early sign, less physical exploration (pointing or asking for objects instead of reaching or climbing for them), trouble drawing simple shapes, building with blocks or Lego and completing puzzles, preferring adults to peers, trouble reading facial expressions or body language, and preferring routine and becoming anxious with change.
Useful to find out: puzzles, blocks and drawing, how the child explores, motor skills compared with language, and reactions to change.

Category: visuospatial_nonverbal | Age band: 4 to 5 years
The same toddler and preschool signs apply: clumsiness, trouble with shapes, blocks and puzzles, awkward crayon and scissor use, trouble with buttons, preferring adults to peers and trouble reading expressions and body language.
Useful to find out: puzzles, drawing and copying shapes, motor tasks, getting lost or confused in familiar places, and play with peers.

Category: visuospatial_nonverbal | Age band: 6 to 11 years
Kindergarten to grade 2: messy letters, poor spacing, slow writing and frustration with drawing; skipping lines, losing their place, trouble copying from the board and tiring quickly with dense worksheets; decoding that is adequate but comprehension that depends on pictures, inference or diagrams that is weak; trouble aligning numbers, understanding place value and number lines and reading operation symbols; trouble telling time on an analog clock; missing jokes or sarcasm, interpreting language literally and struggling with tone; discomfort with group games and looking clumsy on the playground; and anxiety or acting out when routines change.
Grades 3 to 5: trouble grasping meaning from context or visuals in reading, strong ideas but trouble organizing paragraphs, increasing difficulty with place value, multi-step problems, fractions, geometry and graphs, trouble starting tasks, tracking materials, estimating time and monitoring progress without prompts, ongoing clumsiness and bumping into things, missing nonverbal cues, standing too close or too far, and getting lost in a building even after months of visits.
Useful to find out: handwriting and copying, place value and aligning numbers, clock reading, directions and getting lost, organization of materials, and social cues.

Category: visuospatial_nonverbal | Age band: 12 to 17 years
Middle school: trouble tracking assignments, materials and due dates across classes and disorganized lockers and backpacks; trouble starting and finishing work independently and changing strategy when stuck; trouble with longer texts, taking notes from slides and writing structured essays with evidence; challenges with geometry, graphs, charts, lab procedures and multi-step calculations; trouble detecting sarcasm, inside jokes, group cues and subtle exclusion; unstructured times may feel overwhelming; and anxiety or other mental health concerns can develop in students who feel constantly behind or misunderstood. Avoiding groups or events such as dances may look like social anxiety but may stem from visual-spatial overload.
High school: trouble analyzing themes, interpreting figurative language and synthesizing sources; trouble with research papers and lab reports, and deciding what matters most; challenges with matrices, trigonometry, statistics, geometry proofs and multi-step labs; difficulty managing long-term projects and multiple deadlines, estimating time, breaking down tasks and self-advocating; route-finding difficulties, time management problems, trouble remembering materials, and harder learning to drive because of weak spatial judgment; and trouble keeping up with fast conversations, with anxiety, low mood or social isolation. Students diagnosed in high school often have a history of feeling misunderstood.
Useful to find out: organization, deadlines and long-term projects, math and science difficulty, navigation and driving readiness, social cues and mood.
